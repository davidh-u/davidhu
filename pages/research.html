Research

Our research investigates how subducting slabs deliver volatiles and metals to the mantle, whether arc magmas become fertile enough to form ore deposits, how Earth built its first continents, and whether a planet can sustain subduction at all. We couple thermodynamic and geochemical modeling with field-based petrology, geochemistry, and geochronology of natural rocks, linking the mineral-scale record to plate- and planetary-scale processes.

Porphyry Cu deposits

Copper is considered a critical or strategic mineral by several major economies, and porphyry copper deposits supply most of the world's copper, along with significant molybdenum, rhenium, and gold. These deposits are linked to oxidized and H2O-rich arc magmas, but where and when magmas acquire these characteristics remains debated.

Using thermodynamic modeling, we showed that an initially oxidized mantle source is required to generate porphyry-forming magmas, and that wet to super-wet magmatism (H2O > 6 wt%) best reproduces the compositional vectors used to identify fertile systems. This work challenges the widely cited model in which deep garnet fractionation drives magma oxidation. Our recent work also shows that the Ti content of zircon, a proxy increasingly used to identify ore-forming magmas, is controlled by several magmatic factors besides H2O.

We test these ideas in natural systems through an NSF-funded project in Sonora, Mexico, one of the largest copper-producing regions in the world. Zircon petrochronology shows that ore-related porphyries crystallized from more hydrous and oxidized magmas than barren intrusions, in two discrete pulses of fertile magmatism (c. 80–74 Ma and 59–55 Ma), and that Sonoran deposits share the zircon signatures of giant Andean deposits. The group is now studying the role of magmatic cumulates in thickened crust, titanite and apatite petrochronology, and orogenic gold systems, and, with colleagues, the El Teniente supergiant deposit in Chile. We are also extending this work to island arcs (El Arco, Baja California) and to other critical elements such as scandium.

Subduction zones

Subducting slabs carry water and trace elements into the mantle, and the reactions that release them control arc magmatism, geochemical cycling, and seismicity. Our earlier work quantified slab devolatilization and melting, and showed that lawsonite eclogite, a major carrier of H2O and trace elements into the deep mantle, forms only in mature, cold subduction zones.

We test these models against deeply subducted rocks exhumed around the world, from the Acatlán Complex (Mexico) and the North Qaidam terrane (China) to Cedros Island and the Puerto Nuevo mélange in Baja California, which preserves the youngest eclogite-facies record in the North American Cordillera. We also study how mineral reactions trigger earthquakes: dehydration of brucite and antigorite reactivated the fault that produced the 2017 Mw 7.1 Puebla–Morelos intermediate-depth earthquake, and talc production along the plate interface beneath Guerrero is linked to slow slip.

Earth's first continents

Zircons in Archean granitoids record relatively oxidizing and wet parental magmas, a signature that has been used to argue that subduction began in the Eoarchean. We showed that such magmas can be generated by partial melting of overthickened mafic crust, without subduction.

Oxidizing and wet signatures are therefore not diagnostic of subduction. Instead, their apparent increase during the Eoarchean may record the progressive magmatic thickening of the crust, a process that likely contributed to the formation of Earth's first continents.

Mars and planetary tectonics

Why does Mars lack subduction-driven plate tectonics? Combining numerical and thermodynamic modeling, we tested whether early water on Mars inhibited long-term subduction.

Dry Martian crust could have subducted along cool geotherms, but minimal hydration (> 0.45 wt% H2O) stabilized low-density hydrous minerals that kept the crust too buoyant to sink. This result holds across the hemispheric difference in crustal thickness and for a thin early lithosphere. Rather than promoting plate tectonics, early water on Mars likely inhibited it.

Ore deposits beyond Earth

On Earth, ore deposits are commonly viewed as a product of plate tectonics. Our work challenges this assumption: oxidized and wet magmas can form without subduction, and Mars had a thick crust and early water but never developed plate tectonics. This raises a fundamental question: can ore-forming magmatic–hydrothermal systems develop on stagnant-lid planets?

We are addressing this question by applying the approach we developed for terrestrial porphyry deposits to Martian compositions and tectonic settings. Our aims are to quantify whether Martian magmas can reach the volatile content, redox state, and metal availability needed to form porphyry- and iron oxide–copper–gold-type systems; to test these predictions against Martian meteorites and orbital and rover observations; and to extend this framework to the Moon and other planetary crusts.
