¡Bienvenidos!

I lead a petrology research group in the Department of Earth and Environmental Sciences at the University of Illinois Chicago. We study what happens to rocks as they are buried, heated, and melted inside planets, and how those processes shape the world around us: how copper deposits form, where subduction-zone earthquakes come from, how Earth grew its first continents, and why Mars never developed plate tectonics.

Our approach pairs thermodynamic modeling, which lets us predict how rocks and magmas behave under conditions we cannot observe directly, with fieldwork and laboratory analyses that put those predictions to the test.

Prospective graduate students and postdoctoral researchers interested in joining the group are encouraged to contact me by email.

What we study

Porphyry Cu deposits

Porphyry copper deposits supply most of the world's copper and are linked to oxidized, water-rich arc magmas. We investigate where and when magmas acquire these characteristics and what distinguishes fertile systems from barren ones, combining thermodynamic modeling with field and analytical studies in Sonora, Mexico, and the Andes.

How Earth built its first continents

Zircons in Archean rocks record oxidized and wet magmas, a signature often used to argue for early subduction. Our work shows that such magmas can also form by melting of overthickened mafic crust without subduction, with implications for how Earth's first continents formed.

Beyond Earth

Mars never developed subduction-driven plate tectonics, and our work points to early water as a key reason. Our models show that even small amounts of water made the Martian crust too light to sink.
